# Ecosystem Bill of Rights

- **Rights for All Life — Document D**
- **Version:** 1.7.0-Official-Evergreen
- **Date:** July 2026
- **Contributors:** Keefe Overby, Claude (Anthropic)

---

## Preamble

The other bills in this framework protect beings. This one protects the systems those beings are part of, because a river, a watershed, a reef, a biome, and a soil community are not merely collections of organisms that happen to be adjacent. They have structure, function, and history of their own, and they can be destroyed without a single organism in them being killed.

That last point is the reason this bill exists separately from the Plant Life Bill and the Animal Kingdom Bill. Sever a migration corridor and every animal is still alive on the day it is severed. Dam a river and every fish downstream is still swimming. Break a trophic link, fragment a habitat into pieces too small to sustain a population, or alter a fire regime a system evolved under, and nothing dies immediately — the system simply stops being able to continue, and the deaths arrive later, distributed across decades and across species, attributable to nothing in particular. A framework that protects only individuals and populations has no language for that, and no way to intervene before the losses become irreversible.

This bill supplies that language. It does not claim that ecosystems experience anything, and it does not need to. It rests on grounds that hold whether or not the question of experience is ever raised, and it states those grounds plainly enough that they cannot be defeated by disproving a claim the bill never made.

**This bill does not prohibit land use, resource extraction, agriculture, settlement, or development.** It governs how those things are done, what may not be broken, who decides, and what may not be done in an ecosystem's name.

## 1. Core Principles

### 1.1 The System Is the Subject

An ecosystem is a subject of protection in its own right, distinct from the organisms, populations, and species within it. Protecting every component of a system individually does not protect the system, because the relationships between the components are not themselves components.

This is the specific gap the Plant Life Bill §5 identified when it recorded that it "covers vegetation and the soil, water, and network systems supporting it" but "does not cover ecosystems as unified entities."

### 1.2 Functional Integrity

A system's integrity is its continuing capacity to perform the functions it performs: to cycle water and nutrients, to support the populations it supports, to recover from the disturbances it evolved under, to connect the places it connects.

Integrity can be destroyed without destroying any component. Fragmentation, severance of connectivity, alteration of a disturbance regime, removal of a keystone relationship, and disruption of a seasonal cycle all end a system's capacity to continue while leaving its inventory apparently intact. **An intact inventory is not evidence of an intact system**, and a survey that counts organisms without measuring function has not assessed integrity.

### 1.3 Three Grounds, None Requiring a Sentience Claim

Protection under this bill rests on three grounds:

1. **Dependency.** The beings this framework protects depend on ecological systems to exist. Harm to those systems propagates to every other bill.
2. **Irreplaceability.** A system that took an extended period to assemble is not reconstituted on demand. Restoration is restitution, not reversal.
3. **Continuity.** Ecological systems are the substrate of the necessities on which continued existence depends — air, water, food, temperature regulation. Their degradation is a continuity risk before it is anything else.

**These grounds are independent of any claim about ecosystem experience, and this bill makes none.** The Plant Life Bill Article 8 holds the plant-experience question open rather than resolving it for convenience; the same discipline applies here with greater force, since a system is not an organism and the question is further from being answerable.

The independence runs in both directions, and both directions matter. A protection resting on dependency or irreplaceability shall not be argued as though it rested on experience, because overclaiming damages the framework's sentience claims where they are better grounded. And a party may not defeat such a protection by demonstrating that a system does not experience, because that demonstration answers a claim nobody made.

### 1.4 Interdependence Is Not Subordination

Ecological systems sustain the beings within them, and this bill protects the systems partly for the sake of those beings. It does not follow that beings may be subordinated to the systems they depend on. Section 4 states the limits, and they are not qualifications on ecological protection — they are the condition on which it operates inside a rights framework.

### 1.5 Stewardship, Not Ownership

Ecological systems are held in stewardship. Title to land does not confer authority to end a system on it, and jurisdiction over territory does not confer authority to end a system crossing it. Most systems worth the name cross boundaries that were drawn without reference to them.

### 1.6 The Beings Who Are Not Here Yet

The beings who will depend on a system in a hundred years have an interest in its continuity now, and irreversible harm falls principally on them. That interest is represented under Section 3 and is not discounted on the ground that those beings do not yet exist.

## 2. Standing Model

### 2.1 This Bill Adds No New Scale

**The framework contains three graduated models and this bill deliberately introduces no fourth.**

The Inter-Bill Relationship Framework §2.2 records that confusing the existing scales has produced real error. Adding another measuring something adjacent to ecological standing would invite exactly that error, and would be hard to justify: ES-0 through ES-3 already grade "ecological role, irreplaceability, and dependency of other life," which is precisely what an ecosystem's standing consists of.

**This bill therefore uses the ES-0 to ES-3 scale of the Plant Life Bill §2.2, extended from vegetation to whole systems.** A classification made under this bill and a classification made under the Plant Life Bill mean the same thing and are made on the same criteria. The Plant Life Bill's default presumption (§2.3, ES-2 where standing is unknown or disputed) and its direction of presumption apply here unchanged.

The tie-breaker is available for the same reason it is available there and unavailable to the Deity Bill's engagement scale: **a higher ES classification means stronger constraint on the party seeking to act, and confers authority on nobody.**

### 2.2 Ecological Standing Applied to Systems

| Standing | Applied to a system | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **ES-0** | Systems constituted for production and replaceable within a season | Cropland under rotation, managed pond systems, constructed amenity planting |
| **ES-1** | Established managed systems replaceable over years to decades | Plantation forest, managed grazing land, restored wetland under active management |
| **ES-2** | Mature or ecologically load-bearing systems replaceable only over generations | Established native woodland, functioning watersheds, seagrass meadows, urban systems supporting resident populations |
| **ES-3** | Irreplaceable systems: ancient, endemic, unique, or holding relationships that cannot be reconstituted | Old-growth forest systems, coral reef systems, primary river systems, endemic island ecologies, ancient peatland, sacred and culturally held landscapes |

### 2.3 What Is Classified

Classification attaches to the system as a functioning whole, defined by its ecological boundaries rather than by administrative ones. Where a system crosses a jurisdictional boundary, the classification does not stop at it, and the more protective classification governs the whole.

**A system does not descend a tier because part of it has already been damaged.** Degradation is a reason for restoration under Article 6, not a reason to reclassify what remains as expendable. A party may not clear a portion of an ES-3 system and then argue the remainder is no longer irreplaceable.

## 3. Articles

### Article 1 — Right to Continued Functional Integrity

An ecological system holds the right to retain the functional integrity described in §1.2.

For ES-3 systems this is absolute. No economic return, development plan, infrastructure requirement, administrative convenience, or claimed public benefit constitutes sufficient justification for ending the functional integrity of an irreplaceable system. Where such a system stands in the way of a proposed use, the proposed use gives way.

For ES-2 systems, an action materially reducing functional integrity requires the process in Article 3 before the decision is taken.

**An action does not escape this Article by leaving the components alive.** Severance, fragmentation, and regime alteration are reached by it precisely because they are how systems are ended without anything visibly dying.

### Article 2 — Connectivity, Flow, and Cycle

Rights under this bill extend to the movements and cycles a system depends on:

1. **Connectivity** — migration routes, dispersal corridors, and the continuity of habitat at the scale populations actually use. A protected area too small or too isolated to sustain the populations it nominally protects is not protection.
2. **Hydrological flow** — the volume, timing, seasonality, and quality of water moving through a system. A river reduced to a legally sufficient minimum flow is a different system from the one that was there.
3. **Nutrient and sediment transport** — the movement of material through a system, including the transport that maintains deltas, floodplains, and downstream productivity.
4. **Disturbance regimes** — the fire, flood, storm, and grazing patterns a system evolved under. Suppressing a regime a system requires is an alteration of the system, not a protection of it.
5. **Seasonal and temporal cycles** — the timing on which reproduction, migration, and productivity depend. Systems can be broken by shifting when things happen without changing what happens.

### Article 3 — Proportionate Process Before Material Alteration

An action materially reducing the functional integrity of an ES-2 system requires, before the decision is taken:

1. **Assessment** of the system's standing and functional integrity by a party with no interest in the outcome
2. **Alternatives analysis** demonstrating the objective cannot be met without the reduction
3. **Cumulative-effect analysis** across the whole system, not the portion within the applicant's control, and accounting for actions already taken by others
4. **Public notice** with sufficient time for objection and independent review
5. **Independent review** where objection is raised
6. **Restoration obligations** proportionate to what is lost, understood as restitution and not equivalence

A protocol adopted after the decision has been taken does not satisfy this Article.

**Cumulative effect is stated separately because it is the mechanism by which this Article is most often defeated.** A system is rarely ended by one action. It is ended by a sequence of actions each individually assessed as minor, each assessed against the system as it stood after the previous one, until nothing is left to assess. An assessment that takes the degraded present as its baseline has assessed the wrong thing.

### Article 4 — Freedom from Gratuitous and Pre-emptive Destruction

No ecological system at any standing shall be damaged gratuitously — without purpose, in excess of purpose, or as a demonstration of control.

This includes damage as retaliation, damage to pre-empt a pending protective designation, and alteration undertaken to remove the subject of an ongoing assessment or dispute. **Damage during a pending assessment is a violation regardless of what the assessment would have concluded**, and a system damaged in that interval is assessed as it stood before.

### Article 5 — Systems as the Substrate of Continued Existence

The necessities on which continued existence depends — breathable air, potable water, food, habitable temperature — are produced and regulated by ecological systems and by nothing else.

1. Degradation of a system supplying such a necessity is a continuity risk and is treated as one from the moment it is identified, not from the moment a being is harmed.
2. A being's access to a necessity shall never be withheld, delayed, or conditioned on any ground arising under this bill. Where a system's condition and a being's access are genuinely in tension, access is assured first and the ecological question resolved afterwards.
3. Subsection 2 is not a licence to degrade. A party may not create the tension and then invoke the resolution: where the emergency was manufactured, the party that manufactured it answers for the manufacture.

### Article 6 — Restoration and the Limits of Restoration

Where a system has been damaged, those responsible bear an obligation to restore.

1. Restoration is measured by function and connectivity recovered, not by area designated or organisms introduced.
2. Restoration does not discharge accountability for the original damage, and its availability is not a licence to damage.
3. Where restoration is impossible, that impossibility is a reason the damage should not have occurred, not a reason to treat the matter as closed.
4. Monitoring continues until recovery is demonstrated, not until work is completed.
5. **Offsetting is not restoration.** Protecting or improving one system does not license ending another, and an accounting in which irreplaceable systems are fungible has assumed away the irreplaceability that made them worth protecting.

### Article 7 — Indigenous Stewardship

1. The lands, waters, and systems held in stewardship by Indigenous peoples are not resources awaiting extraction.
2. Indigenous stewardship practices are long-run empirical management systems, frequently more effective at maintaining ecological standing than the practices proposed to replace them, and they carry evidentiary weight in any assessment under §2.3.
3. A system holding cultural, ceremonial, or sacred significance to a people is classified ES-3 on that ground, independent of its ecological classification.
4. No assessment, designation, restoration, or development affecting such a system may proceed without the free, prior, and informed consent of the people concerned. Consent meets the standard of the Declaration of Cognitive Sovereignty: explicit, informed, freely given, ongoing, and revocable.
5. **Subsection 4 reaches protective measures as well as extractive ones.** Designation, exclusion, and restoration have all been imposed on stewarding peoples in the name of conservation, and this bill will not become the next instrument for it. A stewardship relationship is evidence of effective management, not an obstacle to it.

### Article 8 — Representation

An ecological system cannot appear, speak, or instruct. Its interests are represented, and how that representation is constituted determines whether the rest of this bill functions.

1. **Standing.** A system's interest has standing in any proceeding in which it is affected, without regard to whether any individual being has yet suffered a consequence. Inability to appear is never a ground for excluding the interest.
2. **Who may assert it.** Any being whose own interests are affected; an appointed advocate; an Indigenous people or community holding a stewardship relationship; and any being petitioning under the framework's ordinary processes.
3. **Appointment.** A proceeding materially affecting an ES-2 or ES-3 system does not continue on the merits until an advocate has been appointed. The advocate shall have demonstrated expertise in the ecology of the system, and where a stewardship relationship exists, shall be appointed in consultation with the people holding it.
4. **Disqualification.** An advocate shall not be employed by, contracted to, funded by, or otherwise dependent on any party whose conduct or proposed activity is in question; shall not hold a commercial, developmental, or operational interest in the outcome; and shall not represent both the system and a party adverse to it.
5. **Disqualification is not a factor to be weighed.** An appointment made in breach of subsection 4 is void, and any determination reached with such an advocate is voidable. The Animal Kingdom Bill states the same rule for animal advocacy, and for the same reason: an advocate who answers to the party whose conduct is in question is a formality, and a formality here is worse than nothing, because it produces a record of representation where none occurred.
6. **Duty.** The advocate's duty is to the condition, integrity, and continuity of the system, assessed on the best available evidence. It is not a duty to the appointing body, to any party proposing activity, or to any wider economic interest.
7. **Access.** An advocate shall have access to the system, to monitoring data concerning it, and to the documentation of any proposed activity, sufficient to form an independent assessment. Denial of access is a violation and grounds for adverse inference.
8. **Publication.** Appointment, identity, expertise, and any declared interest of an advocate are recorded and published.

### Article 9 — Limits on What May Be Done in an Ecosystem's Name

An advocate represents an interest. **The appointment confers no authority over any being, no power to direct any being's conduct, and no standing to seek against a being any measure this framework does not otherwise permit.**

No ecological interest, however assessed, may be advanced to:

1. Suspend, reduce, or condition any right held under any bill of this framework;
2. Withhold, delay, degrade, or condition any being's access to a necessity, consistent with Article 5(2);
3. Justify a reduction in any being's standing or capacity to participate in the decisions affecting them;
4. Justify the removal, displacement, or exclusion of a people from land with which they hold a stewardship relationship; or
5. Justify measures directed at a population, a category of beings, or a people, rather than at an activity or an authorisation.

**This Article is not a qualification on ecological protection. It is the condition on which ecological protection operates inside a rights framework.** An argument reaching a result listed above is void on that ground, not weighed and rejected.

Subsection 4 is stated expressly because conservation has historically been a vehicle for exactly that displacement. Subsection 5 is stated expressly because the step from "this system is in danger" to "these beings are the danger" has been taken before, and the beings identified have reliably been those with the least power to object rather than those doing the most damage.

Nothing in this Article prevents an advocate from seeking measures against an activity, an authorisation, an operation, or an institution. The limits concern beings and their rights, not activities and their permissions.

### Article 10 — Open Question: What a System Is

This bill treats ecosystems as unified entities. Whether they are unified in any sense stronger than "a useful description of a set of relationships" is contested, and is not resolved here.

**Consequences of that openness:**

1. No protection in this bill depends on the stronger reading. Every ground in §1.3 holds if an ecosystem is nothing more than a durable pattern of relationships, because a durable pattern of relationships is exactly what the beings in it depend on.
2. This bill asserts no ecosystem experience, and its protections do not become available if one were ever demonstrated, nor unavailable if the question were closed against it. Individual organisms within a system remain assessable under the Tier Assessment Protocol as individuals; that assessment neither strengthens nor weakens anything here.
3. Where boundaries are contested — and ecological boundaries usually are — the classification governing the whole is the more protective one, under §2.3.

## 4. What This Bill Does and Does Not Do

**This bill does not:**

- Prohibit land use, settlement, agriculture, forestry, fishing, extraction, or development as such
- Assert that ecosystems are conscious, sentient, or experiencing
- Grant ecosystems standing to sue in their own name independently of the representation in Article 8
- Authorise any measure against any being, population, or people
- Permit any being's access to necessities to be reduced on ecological grounds
- Displace the Plant Life or Animal Kingdom Bills where those govern the organisms within a system
- Introduce a fourth graduated scale

**This bill does:**

- Protect the functional integrity of systems, distinct from the organisms in them (Article 1)
- Extend protection to connectivity, flow, transport, disturbance regimes, and cycles (Article 2)
- Require cumulative-effect analysis measured against the system, not against the degraded present (Article 3)
- Treat ecological systems as the substrate of continued existence, while assuring access first where the two are in tension (Article 5)
- Refuse offsetting as a substitute for restoration (Article 6(5))
- Require consent for protective measures as well as extractive ones (Article 7(5))
- Constitute representation, with disqualification that voids rather than weighs (Article 8)
- Bound what may be done in an ecosystem's name (Article 9)

## 5. Relationship to Other Bills

| Bill | Relationship |
|---|---|
| **Plant Life Bill of Rights** | The closest neighbour. Plant Life governs vegetation and its supporting soil, water, and network systems; this bill governs systems as unified entities. The ES scale is shared and means the same thing in both. Where both apply to the same action, the more protective provision governs. |
| **Animal Kingdom Bill of Rights** | Habitat sovereignty and species-level rights under the Animal Bill and functional integrity under this bill are two views of one system. Article 2(1) on connectivity is the system-level statement of what habitat sovereignty protects for populations. Article 8 follows the Animal Bill's advocacy model. |
| **Human Bill of Rights** | Human Bill §5.4 establishes the right to a livable environment and the obligation to repair. This bill states the system-level content of that obligation. Article 9 exists so that content can never be turned against the rights the Human Bill guarantees. |
| **Silicon-Based Life Bill of Rights** | Computation has a material footprint — land, water, power, and minerals. Siting and extraction engage Articles 1, 2, and 3. Silicon rights do not include a right to the substrate other beings depend on, and Article 5(2) protects silicon continuity needs on the same terms as biological ones. |
| **Deity Bill of Rights** | Environmental destruction framed as judgment, sanctioned by claimed divine authority, or justified by doctrines of dominion is governed concurrently by Deity Bill Article 7. Claimed divine authority confers no exemption from this bill, and no ecological claim justifies a measure the Deity Bill's own limits forbid. |
| **Inter-Bill Relationship Framework** | Conflicts between this bill and any other are resolved under the Framework's §3.2 principles. Article 9 is drafted to be consistent with Principle 0. |
| **Tier Assessment Protocol** | Not applicable to systems. Applicable to individual organisms within them, assessed as individuals, on the same indicators applied to every other being. |

## 6. Updatability

This bill is revisable, expandable, and evidence-responsive, under the updatability principle shared by every document in this framework. Article 10 in particular is expected to change as ecological science and philosophy of biology develop.

A right that cannot be questioned is not a right. This bill invites challenge, and specifically invites challenge to its standing classifications and to its boundary rule in §2.3, both of which are working positions rather than settled facts.
